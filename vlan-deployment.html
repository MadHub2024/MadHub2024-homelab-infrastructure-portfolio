Case Study 01 · Network Architecture

Enterprise-Style Multi-VLAN Network Segmentation

Replacing a flat network with segmented security zones, centralized DNS, explicit access-control policies, and controlled cross-VLAN service discovery using TP-Link Omada infrastructure.

TP-Link Omada VLANs ACLs Layer 3 routing mDNS Technitium DNS AdGuard Home

Executive summary

The homelab evolved from a small collection of systems into a production-style environment containing infrastructure servers, workstations, IoT devices, media systems, printers, management appliances, and guest clients. Keeping every device on one flat LAN created unnecessary trust relationships and made policy enforcement difficult.

The network was redesigned around separate VLANs, routed through the TP-Link ER7412 and connected by an SX3008F 10 Gb backbone. Explicit ACLs were used to permit required services while denying unnecessary lateral access.

Outcome: More than seven logical network segments now separate management, servers, workstations, IoT, media, printers, guests, and lab systems while preserving required DNS, SSH, AirPrint, AirPlay, Hubitat, and administrative workflows.

Problem

  • IoT and media devices had broader network visibility than required.
  • Management interfaces were not isolated from normal client traffic.
  • Printers and guest clients needed stricter access boundaries.
  • Infrastructure services required selective cross-VLAN communication.
  • AirPrint, AirPlay, HomeKit, and Hubitat depended on controlled mDNS behavior.
  • DNS needed to remain centralized across every routed segment.

Design principles

  1. Separate by trust level Infrastructure, management, user, IoT, printing, guest, and lab systems were placed into distinct broadcast and policy domains.
  2. Default deny between VLANs Cross-VLAN access was blocked unless a documented business or operational requirement justified an explicit rule.
  3. Centralize DNS Every VLAN was directed to Technitium DNS so internal names and filtering followed one consistent path.
  4. Allow narrow service paths Rules were scoped by source, destination, protocol, and port rather than granting broad subnet-to-subnet access.
  5. Preserve service discovery intentionally mDNS reflection was limited to required services such as AirPrint, AirPlay, HomeKit, and Hubitat.

ACL strategy

DNS access

Client VLANs were permitted to reach the designated DNS resolver on TCP and UDP port 53. Direct use of alternate internal resolvers was avoided so troubleshooting and policy enforcement remained consistent.

Administrative access

SSH and management interfaces were restricted to trusted workstation and management sources rather than exposed across every VLAN.

IoT and media isolation

IoT and media devices were denied general access to management, workstation, and server networks. Narrow exceptions were created for services such as Hubitat, Homebridge, DNS, and required streaming or discovery traffic.

Printer policy

Printers were isolated from sensitive systems while approved client networks retained print access. Internet access was limited to what the device required for updates and cloud functionality.

Guest policy

Guest clients received internet access without access to internal infrastructure, management interfaces, or private application services.

mDNS and cross-VLAN discovery

Segmentation breaks multicast-based discovery by design. Required services therefore had to be reintroduced intentionally rather than by weakening the entire policy model.

  • AirPrint advertisements were made available to approved client VLANs.
  • AirPlay discovery was preserved for authorized media devices.
  • Hubitat and HomeKit communication paths were explicitly tested.
  • Bonjour forwarding rules were limited to the services that needed them.

Operational improvements

  • Reduced unnecessary lateral visibility between device classes.
  • Established a repeatable default-deny policy model.
  • Centralized DNS across all routed client networks.
  • Documented service-specific ACL and mDNS requirements.
  • Improved troubleshooting by separating routing, DNS, ACL, and application tests.
  • Created a design that can support future lab and business-service growth.

Lessons learned

  • Document required traffic before enabling default deny.
  • Validate DNS before assuming an application is unavailable.
  • Test allowed and denied paths from the actual source VLAN.
  • mDNS should be treated as an explicit exception, not a reason to flatten the network.
  • Small, source-specific rules are easier to audit than broad subnet permissions.

Lessons learned

  • Default-deny segmentation works best when required traffic flows are documented before ACL deployment.
  • mDNS and Bonjour must be treated as intentional exceptions rather than reasons to weaken segmentation.
  • Testing from the actual source VLAN is essential because local tests can hide routing or ACL failures.
  • DNS, routing, ACLs, and application readiness should be validated as separate layers.

What I would do differently

I would stage the VLAN rollout in smaller maintenance windows, maintain a formal source-to-destination traffic matrix, and validate each permit and deny rule with scripted tests. I would also export controller configuration before every major ACL or VLAN change.
